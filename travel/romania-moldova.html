The eleven eras, by number of entries

All 12 World Heritage properties across the two countries are tagged and all 23 tentative-list entries are represented. Nineteen entries have no Lonely Planet listing and were added as rows of their own, marked “not in Lonely Planet” — Horezu, Roșia Montană, Ulpia Traiana Sarmizegetusa, the Struve arc, the Domogled beech forests, Basarabi-Murfatlar, the Oltenian culă manors, Densuș, Pietrosul Rodnei, Sânpetru, Rimetea, the Danube limes, the Movile and Coliboaia caves, the Cucuteni–Trypillia sites in both countries, the Bălți chernozem steppe and the Cricova and Mileștii Mici wine cellars. Roșia Montană was inscribed in 2021 directly onto the List of World Heritage in Danger, where it remains — the only property here with that status. Neither country has ever had a property delisted. The era list supplied runs from the steppe peoples straight to the Ottomans with nothing for the centuries between, so the Magyar band is stretched to 1526 and carries the whole Hungarian medieval period in Transylvania, Banat, Crișana and Maramureș — the Saxon fortified churches, Bran, Corvin Castle, the Sighișoara guild towers — while the Ottoman band runs from 1396 in Wallachia and Moldavia and carries the princely foundations, built by Romanian voivodes paying tribute rather than by Ottomans. Austro-Hungarian and Kingdom of Romania overlap for half a century and are split by region rather than by date: the four Habsburg regions use the first, the four Old Kingdom regions the second, and from 1918 everything is Union. Bessarabia is populated entirely by Moldova’s listings, and southern Bucovina is filed under Moldavia since the region list has no Bukovina. Three systematic estimates matter: an undated museum or memorial is Union; an undated church, monastery or fortress is Magyar in the Hungarian regions and Ottoman in the principalities; an undated castle or townhouse is Austro-Hungarian in the Hungarian regions and Kingdom of Romania in the Old Kingdom. Lonely Planet’s Romanian coverage is heavily institutional — 120 museums against a single Roman site, the Constanța mosaic — and generic names abound, with four Roman Catholic Cathedrals, three Orthodox Cathedrals, three statues of Mihai Eminescu and two Palaces of Culture, so every Wikipedia candidate was checked against its own opening paragraph and 48 false matches were dropped. One dating trap is worth recording: the word Roman appears as Roman Catholic in nearly every Transylvanian church article, which dated eighteen fortified churches to the second century until the rule was removed. The area inscriptions — Sighișoara, the Danube Delta, the fortified church villages, the Maramureș wooden churches, the Churches of Moldavia — are tagged at destination level. Map links are searches, not pins: Lonely Planet publishes no coordinates.
