The eleven eras, by number of entries

Built from Lonely Planet’s own points-of-interest index for Iceland — 399 listings — across 137 destinations, grouped as Reykjavík plus Suðurnes, Vesturland, Vestfirðir, Norðurland vestra, Norðurland eystra, Austurland and Suðurland. That is close to Lonely Planet’s own chapter structure, so only 37 chapter-level sights needed placing by hand. The headline fact about this atlas is the band that is **not** here. Iceland was uninhabited until about 870, so there is no Prehistory band at all — the first era is Settlement, and the whole human record of the island fits into eleven centuries. The second fact is what Iceland built in, and what that did to the middle bands. Turf and driftwood do not survive, and the oldest intact building in the country is Viðeyjarstofa of 1755. So **Norwegian Rule holds nothing at all**, **Danish Rule holds one** entry and the **Trade Monopoly four**. That is not a gap in the data: between 1262 and 1786 Iceland produced almost no structure that still stands and that Lonely Planet lists. The one Danish Rule entry is **Skriðuklaustur**, whose monastery was founded in 1493 and excavated in the 2000s; the Trade Monopoly holds Viðeyjarstofa, Hólar cathedral of 1763, the Núpsstaður turf chapel and the Skansinn fort on Heimaey. **Settlement** holds four and the **Commonwealth** nine, and both depend on documented exceptions. Under the series rule a museum is dated by its own founding, which would put the Settlement Exhibition, Eiírksstaðir, Stöng and the Þjóðveldisbærinn reconstruction in the twentieth century. They are filed instead in the era of the farm beneath them — as Kierikki was in Finland and the Hunebedcentrum in the Netherlands — and 21 entries in all were set by hand. **Natural takes 164 entries, 41% of the table**, the largest share anywhere in this series. That is Iceland: the waterfalls, glaciers, volcanoes, lava fields, geothermal areas, fjords and black-sand coasts are the sights. To keep the band legible the natural *types* are split twelve ways — Waterfall, Glacier or icecap, Volcano or crater, Lava field, cave or fissure, Geothermal area or hot spring, Mountain or hill, Coast, beach or cliff, River or canyon, Lake or lagoon, Fjord or bay, Island or archipelago, Nature reserve or national park — so the Site type filter does the work the era filter cannot. All 3 inscribed World Heritage properties are tagged — Þingvellir, Surtsey and Vatnajökull — and 4 of the 6 tentative-list entries. **Surtsey** has no Lonely Planet listing, because the island is closed to visitors, and was added as a row marked “not in Lonely Planet”. The two tentative entries that show no rows are both Þingvellir under other names — *Viking Monuments and Sites* and a tentative extension — and are superseded by the 2004 inscription, which takes precedence in the tagging. **The Turf House Tradition** is the tentative entry that earns its place: eleven rows, from Glaumbær and Laufás to Keldur, Bustarfell and Núpsstaður. Iceland has never had a property delisted. Area inscriptions are tagged at destination level, which is why Vatnajökull shows 15 rows and Mývatn and Laxá fourteen. 150 entries take their era from their type alone, besides the 164 that are Natural by type: an undated settlement site is Settlement; a monastery is Commonwealth; a cathedral is Trade Monopoly; a church, turf farm, old house, old quarter or street is Free Trade & Awakening, because almost every parish church and turf farm standing in Iceland was built between 1786 and 1874; a civic building, lighthouse, harbour or park is Home Rule; a works or fort is Kingdom & Occupation; a museum, open-air museum, theatre, zoo, pool, viewpoint, statue, shop, theme park or bridge is Republic & Cold War; an art gallery is Modern. 39 eras come from a Wikidata date, 21 from the opening paragraph of the sight’s own article, five from a period cue, and 21 were set by hand. Wikipedia was resolved by direct lookup, then the langlinks of the Icelandic Wikipedia, then Icelandic-to-English name forms, then a full-text search, and then a hand pass of 215 candidate titles of which 141 existed. The middle waves were thin again — 12 articles from langlinks and 2 from name forms — and a final check of every picked article dropped a further 37 that turned out not to exist: **201 of 400 rows have an English article of their own**, and the balance is lopsided, because Iceland’s waterfalls, glaciers and volcanoes are individually notable while its churches, turf farms and local museums almost never are. Every candidate was checked against its own opening paragraph for an Icelandic cue and rejected without one, which caught Harpa resolving to a plant genus, Tvísöngur to the musical interval, Dómkirkja to the general article on cathedrals, and Ráðhús to Copenhagen. 155 picks were repaired by hand. Map links are searches, not pins: Lonely Planet publishes no coordinates.
