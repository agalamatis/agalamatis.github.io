The thirteen eras, by number of entries

Built from Lonely Planet’s own points-of-interest index for Norway — 514 listings — across 145 destinations and all 17 counties. The regions are the traditional landsdeler: Oslo, Østlandet, Sørlandet, Vestlandet, Trøndelag and Nord-Norge, with Svalbard separate. That is deliberately not Lonely Planet’s own chapter structure, which files Trondheim and Røros under “Northern Norway”; both sit in Trøndelag here. Five inland national parks that Lonely Planet files under the wrong chapter — Dovrefjell-Sunndalsfjella, Dovre, Reinheimen, Breheimen and Namsskogan — are corrected to Innlandet, Vestland and Trøndelag. All eight inscribed World Heritage properties are tagged and all six tentative-list entries. The Struve Geodetic Arc is carried by the Meridian Monument in Hammerfest, one of its four Norwegian points. One tentative entry has no Lonely Planet listing and was added as a row marked “not in Lonely Planet”: the Jan Mayen and Bouvet nomination for the Mid-Atlantic Ridge. Norway has never had a property delisted, so there is no former-site category here. The area inscriptions and the broad tentative entries are tagged at destination level, which is why The Lofoten Islands shows 26 rows and Svalbard Archipelago 19; Heddal Stave Church is deliberately not tagged with the Rjukan–Notodden industrial inscription although Lonely Planet files it under Notodden. On the eras: your Iron Age span (500 BC – AD 800) contained the Migration Age and overlapped the Viking Age, so at your direction the three are made sequential on your own dates — Iron Age 500 BC to AD 21, Migration Age 21 to 700, Viking Age 700 to 1000 — and every date lands in exactly one band. A sight sits in the band its founding year falls in, not the band of the fabric standing today, and a museum is dated by its own founding rather than by what it holds: the Museum of the Viking Age is Modern, the Oseberg ship inside it is not. One exception is documented: Alta Museum is dated to its rock art, about 5200 BC, because the listing is the carving field at Hjemmeluft and the World Heritage property is the carvings. Norway’s pre-Viking archaeology reaches this table as three entries — the Alta rock art in Prehistory, Ceavccageađgi at Mortensnes in the Bronze Age, and the Mølen cairn field in the Iron Age — and the Migration Age band is empty, which is reported rather than hidden: the Snartemo sword, the Åker buckle and the boat graves are museum collections, and Avaldsnes, which does reach that far back, is listed as a Viking farm and a modern history centre. The Viking Age itself carries only six entries for the same reason — the Oseberg and Gokstad ships are exhibits in Oslo — so what is left is the ground: Kaupang, the Eidfjord and Balestrand barrows, Avaldsnes and the reconstructed hall at Lofotr. The Late Middle Ages, at five entries, shows what the Black Death and the Kalmar Union did to Norwegian building. 294 entries take their era from their type alone: an undated stave church, cathedral or monastery is High Middle Ages; an undated church, fort, manor, trading quarter or mine is Denmark–Norway; an undated street, lighthouse, bridge, park or hydro site is Union with Sweden; an undated museum, open-air museum, statue or railway is Independence & the Wars; an undated art gallery, aquarium or Sámi centre is Oil Industry. A further 71 natural features are Natural by type, 168 eras come from a Wikidata date, 39 from the opening paragraph of the sight’s own article, and eight were set by hand. Wikipedia was resolved by direct lookup, then the langlinks of the Bokmål and Nynorsk Wikipedias, then Norwegian-to-English name forms (Stavkirke to Stave Church, Festning to Fortress, Fyr to Lighthouse, Breen to Glacier), then a full-text search. Every candidate was checked against its own opening paragraph for a Norwegian cue, which caught Trondheim’s Leif Ericson Statue resolving to the one in Seattle, Lillehammer’s Olympic Park to the generic article, Svolvær’s Magic Ice to the Eurasian eagle-owl and Vega’s E-huset to a list of film awards; 24 picks were repaired by hand. English coverage of small Norwegian sights is thin, so 230 of 515 rows have an article of their own and the rest point at the town, the municipality or the institution that covers them, shown with a lower-case w. Map links are searches, not pins: Lonely Planet publishes no coordinates.
