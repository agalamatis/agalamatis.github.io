The ten eras, by number of entries

Built from Lonely Planet’s own points-of-interest index for the Netherlands — 571 listings — across 106 destinations. The regions are the two metros you asked for — Amsterdam with Haarlem, Zaanstad, Almere and Amstelveen; Rotterdam with The Hague, Delft, Zoetermeer and Dordrecht — and the ten historical lordships for everything else: Holland, Utrecht, Guelders, Overijssel, Brabant, Zeeland, Frisia, Groningen, Drenthe and Limburg. That is not Lonely Planet’s own chapter structure, which uses Amsterdam, Rotterdam, the Randstad, the Delta, the North and East and the Southeast, and files 37 sights on chapter pages with no town; those were placed by hand. Two of the lordship labels are looser than they look. **Limburg** here follows the modern province, because the medieval map of that corner is a patchwork — Roermond and Venlo were Upper Guelders, Valkenburg was one of the Lands of Overmaas, Maastricht was a condominium shared with the Prince-Bishop of Liège, and the Duchy of Limburg proper lay mostly in what is now Belgium. **Brabant** likewise follows North Brabant rather than the whole duchy, whose southern half is Belgian. Reclaimed **Flevoland** has no lordship at all: Urk and Schokland went with Overijssel and are filed there, Lelystad with them, and Almere sits in the Amsterdam metro as you asked. Oudewater, a Holland exclave inside Utrecht, is filed under Holland, as it was. All 12 inscribed World Heritage properties of the European Netherlands are tagged. Three have no Lonely Planet listing anywhere and were added as rows marked “not in Lonely Planet”: the Woudagemaal steam pumping station at Lemmer, the Beemster polder, and the Colonies of Benevolence at Frederiksoord and Veenhuizen. The Wadden Sea is inscribed jointly with Germany and Denmark, the Lower German Limes with Germany, and the Colonies of Benevolence with Belgium. The Netherlands has never had a property delisted, so there is no former-site category here. Three further properties of the Kingdom lie in the Caribbean and fall outside this atlas: the inscribed Historic Area of Willemstad on Curaçao, and the two tentative-list entries, Bonaire Marine Park and Plantations in West Curaçao — which is why the tentative count here is zero rather than missing. Area inscriptions are tagged at destination level, and one of them dominates: the seventeenth-century canal ring carries 38 rows, every sight Lonely Planet files in the Western and Southern Canal Rings, whether or not each building is named in the inscription. The Medieval Centre is deliberately **not** tagged, because the property begins outside the Singel. The fortress town of Naarden is tagged whole, as a component of the New Dutch Waterline. On the eras: a sight sits in the band its founding year falls in, not the band of the fabric standing today, and a museum is dated by its own founding rather than by what it holds. One exception is recorded: the **Hunebedcentrum** at Borger is filed in Prehistory, because it stands on D27, the largest hunebed in the country. Three bands are nearly empty, and honestly so. **Prehistory** holds one entry, **Roman** two and the **Frankish Empire** one. The Netherlands has all three in quantity — 54 hunebedden in Drenthe, a Roman frontier that is itself World Heritage, Carolingian Nijmegen and Utrecht — but Lonely Planet lists almost none of it as a visitable site. What survives in the table is the Borger hunebed centre; DOMunder and the Valkhof, the two Lower German Limes components; and the Basilica of Saint Servatius at Maastricht, founded about 570 over the saint’s grave. Your ninth band is labelled Second World and the Cold War but dated 1945–1991, so the war itself falls in **Kingdom**, which runs to 1945: the Anne Frank House, the Westerbork and Vught camps and the Arnhem battlefields are all filed there, and the band from 1945 holds the reconstruction, the museums and the memorials that followed. 268 entries take their era from their type alone: an undated church, cathedral, monastery, castle or city gate is Holy Roman Empire, unless its name says *nieuwe kerk*, in which case it is The Empire; an undated old town or tower is Burgundians & Habsburgs; an undated canal, hofje, canal house, palace, windmill, square, harbour, fort or town hall is The Empire; an undated park, works, lighthouse, bridge, railway or cemetery is Kingdom; an undated museum, statue, theatre, zoo, sports ground, theme park or ship is Second World & Cold War; an art gallery or shop is Modern. A further 25 natural features are Natural by type, 210 eras come from a Wikidata date, 57 from the opening paragraph of the sight’s own article, one from a period cue, and ten were set by hand. Period cues are deliberately **not** applied to museums and visitor centres. Wikipedia was resolved by direct lookup, then the langlinks of the Dutch Wikipedia, then Dutch-to-English name forms, then a full-text search. The name-form wave is worth a note: it returned **nothing**, because English Wikipedia keeps Dutch sights under their Dutch names — Rijksmuseum, Vondelpark, Westerkerk, Binnenhof — rather than translating or disambiguating them, so “Grote Kerk, Netherlands” and its kind do not exist. Every candidate was checked against its own opening paragraph for a Dutch cue and rejected without one, which is what caught Stadhuis resolving to the Altes Stadthaus in Berlin, Waag to the general article on weigh houses, Electric Ladyland to the Jimi Hendrix album, and a long tail of sights resolving to “List of museums in the Netherlands”; 74 picks were repaired by hand, including the Utrecht Domkerk, which had resolved to the general article on cathedrals. 321 of 574 rows have an article of their own and the rest point at the town, the neighbourhood or the institution that covers them, shown with a lower-case w. Map links are searches, not pins: Lonely Planet publishes no coordinates.
