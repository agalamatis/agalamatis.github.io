The nine eras, by number of entries

Built from Lonely Planet’s own points-of-interest index for Finland — 412 listings — across 110 destinations, grouped as Helsinki plus the nine historical provinces: Uusimaa, Finland Proper, Satakunta, Tavastia, Savonia, Karelia, Ostrobothnia, Åland and Lapland. That is not Lonely Planet’s own chapter structure, which uses the modern regions and files a long tail of sights on chapter pages with no town; 33 of those were placed by hand. Note that the historical provinces cut across the modern ones: Jyväskylä and Petäjävesi sit in Tavastia, Tampere in Satakunta, Tornio and Ylitornio in Ostrobothnia rather than Lapland. Åland is a separate jurisdiction — an autonomous, Swedish-speaking region of Finland — and has its own filter, which is why its sights are named in Swedish throughout. All 8 inscribed World Heritage properties are tagged, including the newly inscribed Aalto Works (2026), and the single tentative-list entry, The Ringed Seal Archipelagos of Lake Saimaa, carried by the Linnansaari and Kolovesi national parks. Three properties have no Lonely Planet listing anywhere — Verla Groundwood and Board Mill, the Kvarken Archipelago and the six Finnish points of the Struve Geodetic Arc — and were added as rows marked “not in Lonely Planet”. Kvarken is inscribed jointly with Sweden’s High Coast and the Struve arc with nine other states. Finland has never had a property delisted, so there is no former-site category here. Area inscriptions are tagged at the level of the named component, which is why Old Rauma shows seven rows and Suomenlinna seven; Sammallahdenmäki is in Rauma municipality but is a property of its own and is tagged as such. Lonely Planet reaches only seven of the thirteen Aalto Works components — the Säynätsalo town hall, the Muuratsalo experimental house, Villa Mairea and four buildings of the Seinäjoki Aalto Centre; Finlandia Hall, the Aalto house and atelier, the House of Culture, the Social Insurance Institution office, Sunila, Paimio Sanatorium, the Church of the Three Crosses and the Jyväskylä campus are not listed by Lonely Planet and are not given rows of their own, since the property itself is represented. On the eras: a sight sits in the band its founding year falls in, not the band of the fabric standing today, and a museum is dated by its own founding rather than by what it holds — the Viking-themed exhibits in Finnish museums do not pull them into the Viking Age. One exception is recorded: **Kierikkikeskus**, the Kierikki Stone Age Centre at Yli-Ii, is filed in Prehistory because it is a reconstructed Stone Age village on its own excavated site. Two bands are nearly empty, and honestly so. **Prehistory** holds two entries and the **Viking Age** one. Finland has plenty of both — Comb Ceramic settlement, Bronze Age cairns, Iron Age hill forts, the Viking-era trade of the Åland and Satakunta coasts — but almost none of it reaches a Lonely Planet listing as a visitable site; it reaches visitors as museum collections, and those are dated by the museums. The exceptions are Sammallahdenmäki, the Bronze Age cairn field that is a World Heritage property, Kierikki, and the Hauensuoli rock inscriptions at Hanko. The **Kalmar Union** band holds four: Finland under the union built almost nothing new that survives as a sight, and its medieval stone churches are dated either side of 1397–1521. 207 entries take their era from their type alone: an undated church or cathedral is Middle Ages when its dedication is to a saint and its province is Åland or Finland Proper, Swedish Rule otherwise, and Russian Rule when its name says *uusi* or *nya*, new; an undated monastery or cathedral is Middle Ages; an undated castle, fort or old town is Swedish Rule; an undated manor, harbour, square, park, ironworks, lighthouse, canal, railway, tower or civic building is Russian Rule; an undated museum, open-air museum, statue, theatre, zoo, sports ground or theme park is Independence; an art gallery, market or Sámi centre is Modern. A further 49 natural features are Natural by type, 121 eras come from a Wikidata date, 20 from the opening paragraph of the sight’s own article, two from a period cue, and 13 were set by hand. Period cues are deliberately **not** applied to museums and visitor centres. Wikipedia was resolved by direct lookup, then the langlinks of the Finnish and Swedish Wikipedias, then Finnish- and Swedish-to-English name forms (Tuomiokirkko to Cathedral, Linna to Castle, Kirkko and Kyrka to Church, Ulkomuseo to Open-air museum), then a full-text search. Every candidate was checked against its own opening paragraph for a Finnish cue and rejected without one; 19 picks were repaired by hand. English coverage of small Finnish sights is thin, so 151 of 418 rows have an article of their own and the rest point at the town, the municipality or the institution that covers them, shown with a lower-case w. Map links are searches, not pins: Lonely Planet publishes no coordinates.
