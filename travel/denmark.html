The fifteen eras, by number of entries

Built from Lonely Planet’s own points-of-interest indexes — 403 listings for Denmark, 35 for Greenland and 15 for the Faroes — across 117 destinations. The regions are the historical lands rather than the five administrative regions of 2007: Copenhagen, Zealand, Funen, Jutland, Bornholm and Lolland–Falster–Møn, with the Faroes and Greenland as regions of their own. Møn is filed with Lolland and Falster although Lonely Planet files it under Zealand, Christiansø with Bornholm, and the Wadden Sea islands with Jutland; the Jurisdiction filter separates Denmark proper from the Faroes and Greenland. All 13 inscribed World Heritage properties of the Kingdom are tagged, including the three in Greenland, and all four tentative-list entries. Seven have no Lonely Planet listing and were added as rows marked “not in Lonely Planet”: the par force hunting landscape, the bony fish fossils of the western Limfjord (inscribed in 2026, the newest property here), Kujataa, Aasivissuit–Nipisat, Dragør old town, the Læsø seaweed houses and the workers’ assembly halls. Møns Klint was inscribed in 2025; the Wadden Sea is shared with Germany and the Netherlands; and Christiansfeld, inscribed in 2015, became part of a transnational Moravian property in 2024 that now also includes Gracehill in Northern Ireland and Herrnhut in Germany. Denmark has never had a property delisted, so there is no former-site category here. On the eras: a sight sits in the band its founding year falls in, not the band of the fabric standing today, and a museum about an older period is filed by its own founding date rather than its subject — the Viking Ship Museum is Cold War, the ships inside it are not. Where your bands overlap, the later one takes the boundary: the Estridsen era runs to 1397, the Kalmar Union from 1397 to 1520, the Reformation 1520 to 1536. Five bands carry nothing, and that is reported rather than hidden. The Nordic Bronze Age, Pre-Roman Iron Age, Roman Iron Age and Germanic Iron Age are empty because Denmark’s prehistory reaches the visitor through collections rather than sites: the Sun Chariot and the lurs are in Nationalmuseet, the Tollund and Grauballe men in Museum Silkeborg and Moesgaard, the Hjortspring boat and the Illerup weapon sacrifices in museums too, and Lonely Planet lists the museums, not the bogs and barrows. The Reformation band is sixteen years long and the Danish Reformation closed monasteries rather than founding sights. The nearest miss is Lindholm Høje, whose cemetery begins in the Germanic Iron Age but is filed in the Viking Age, as its own article dates it. Of the rest, 211 entries take their era from their type alone: an undated church, cathedral, abbey or castle ruin is Estridsen era; an undated castle, manor, street, gate or fort is Denmark–Norway; an undated museum, town hall, mill or lighthouse is Industrialisation & World Wars; an undated art gallery, folk museum, zoo or sculpture is Cold War. A further 37 natural features are Natural by type, 163 eras come from a Wikidata date, 72 from the opening paragraph of the sight’s own article, and six from a period cue. Wikipedia was resolved by looking up the Danish name, then following the langlinks of the Danish Wikipedia — which is what turns Besættelsesmuseet into the Occupation Museum, Marmorkirken into Frederik’s Church and Den Blå Planet into the National Aquarium Denmark — then Danish-to-English name forms (Kirke to Church, Slot to Palace, Rundkirke to Round Church), then a full-text search. Every candidate was checked against its own opening paragraph for a Danish, Faroese or Greenlandic cue, which caught Nordkraft resolving to a Norwegian power company, Naturama to a widescreen film process, Copenhagen’s Latin Quarter to the one in Paris and Zealand’s Trelleborg to the Swedish port town; thirty picks were repaired by hand. Danish sights are thinly covered in English, so 265 of 460 rows have an article of their own and the rest point at the town or institution that covers them, shown with a lower-case w. One dating rule is worth recording: period cues are not applied to museums and visitor centres, because a Viking museum’s name and article are full of Viking-Age language — without it, four Viking museums landed in the ninth century. Map links are searches, not pins: Lonely Planet publishes no coordinates.
