The eleven eras, by number of entries

All 10 of Bulgaria’s World Heritage properties are tagged and all 16 tentative-list entries are represented. Six entries have no Lonely Planet listing and were added as rows of their own, marked “not in Lonely Planet” — Pirin National Park, the Central Balkan beech forests, Pobiti Kamani, the Alexandrovo Thracian tomb and the Bishop’s Basilica of Philippopolis. No Bulgarian property has ever been delisted. Bulgaria’s 1984 tentative submissions carry reference numbers that overlap the inscribed numbers of 1979 and 1985 — that is UNESCO’s own numbering, not an error here. The era list supplied is contiguous and non-overlapping, so assignment is a single date ladder. Two edges are worth noting. The fourth band was supplied as Greek, 547 BC to AD 46, and has been renamed Thracian at the user’s request: every entry that falls in it is Thracian rather than Greek colonial — the Kazanlak, Sveshtari, Ostrusha, Shushmanets and Alexandrovo tombs and the Thracian hilltop settlement under Plovdiv — because Lonely Planet lists Nesebar, Sozopol and Pomorie through their medieval churches rather than their Greek foundations. The Roman band runs to 681, so it carries Late Antiquity with it and the First Bulgarian Empire begins exactly where the Bulgar state does. Three systematic estimates matter here. An undated church is filed as Ottoman, because most surviving Bulgarian churches are National Revival rebuildings rather than medieval survivals — except in Nesebar and Veliko Tarnovo, where the default is Second Bulgarian Empire. An undated monastery is Second Bulgarian Empire, because Bulgarian monasteries almost all claim a medieval foundation. An undated museum or gallery is Modern, because most of Bulgaria’s regional museums were founded after 1945. This is the smallest country in the series — 328 Lonely Planet listings — and the one where English Wikipedia helps least: there are nine separate Historical Museums here, four Ethnographic Museums and dozens of churches called some variant of Sveti Nikolai, and English Wikipedia has no article for most Bulgarian regional museums and Revival houses. Every candidate was checked against its own opening paragraph and 24 false matches were dropped — Riga’s Freedom Monument, a Polish rock band called Apteka, the Denver Museum of Nature and Science — leaving 83 of 334 rows with an article of their own, the lowest proportion in this series. The oblast grouping has consequences: Veliko Tarnovo and Gabrovo are in Lovech, Stara Zagora and Kazanlak in Haskovo, Ruse and Silistra in Razgrad, Shumen and Balchik in Varna, and Rila Monastery, Melnik, Bansko and Koprivshtitsa all in Sofia province. The area inscriptions — Nesebar, Rila Monastery, Ivanovo, Srebarna — are tagged at destination level. Map links are searches, not pins: Lonely Planet publishes no coordinates.
