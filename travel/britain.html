The eleven eras, by number of entries

Built from Lonely Planet’s own destination chapters — 2,877 entries across 722 destinations and 41 chapters, reconciled against English Wikipedia and the World Heritage list. Every one of the 29 inscribed properties with a component in Great Britain or the Isle of Man is tagged, on 229 rows, together with all four of the United Kingdom’s current tentative-list entries — the historic core of York, Birkenhead Park, the England East Coast Wetlands and Iron Age Shetland — on 24 rows. The one property Britain has lost is tagged as such: Liverpool – Maritime Mercantile City, inscribed in 2004 and delisted in 2021, still carries twelve sights, marked WHS ×. The Great Spa Towns of Europe is inscribed for Bath but has no Lonely Planet sight of its own, so it was added as a row marked “not in Lonely Planet”. Four British-sounding properties are deliberately absent because nothing in them lies in the area covered here: the Giant’s Causeway and the Moravian settlement at Gracehill are in Northern Ireland, Gorham’s Cave is in Gibraltar, and St George in Bermuda, Gough and Inaccessible Islands, Henderson Island and the Little Cayman marine parks are overseas territories. Two errors in the source chapters were corrected: six Bath sights — the Roman Baths, the Pump Room, Bath Abbey, Pulteney Bridge, the Victoria Art Gallery and the Herschel Museum — were filed under Bristol and are restored to Bath with the City of Bath inscription, and Segedunum at Wallsend was untagged although it is part of the Frontiers of the Roman Empire. On the eras: the bands supplied are contiguous, so a sight sits in the band its founding year falls in, and a date is the founding or earliest-recorded date, not the date of the building standing today — Westminster Abbey is Plantagenet for Henry III’s church rather than Tudor for its completion, and a Norman castle rebuilt as a Georgian house stays with the house. The thin bands are reported rather than hidden: Celtic carries 15 entries and Roman 44, which says more about Lonely Planet’s coverage than about Britain — brochs, hillforts and Pictish stones are scattered through chapters that lead on castles and museums, and Hadrian’s Wall supplies a good share of the Roman entries on its own. Prehistory, at 58, is carried by Wessex and Orkney. Five systematic estimates matter, since 728 entries take their era from their type alone: an undated museum or gallery is Colonial, on the ground that most were Victorian foundations; an undated church or chapel is Plantagenet; an undated castle, cathedral or abbey is Norman; an undated country house, mill, bridge or street is Colonial; and a visitor or heritage centre is Modern. A further 502 natural features — coasts, hills, lochs, caves and woods — are Natural by type. Of the rest, 955 eras come from a Wikidata inception, official-opening or earliest-record date, 606 from the opening paragraph of the sight’s own Wikipedia article, 76 from a period cue such as Georgian or Iron Age, and ten were set by hand. Lonely Planet names many sights by a single generic word, which makes a direct Wikipedia lookup dangerous: the Gherkin resolved to pickled cucumber, Glastonbury’s White Spring to a K-pop discography, Lerwick’s Knab to a Dutch insurer and Helmsdale’s Timespan to the article on eras. Every candidate was tested against its own opening paragraph for a British cue; 67 failed. Twenty-seven were repointed at the right article — the Gherkin to 30 St Mary Axe, Plymouth’s Barbican and Royal Citadel, Oxford’s Bridge of Sighs to Hertford Bridge, Leicester’s Magazine to the Magazine Gateway — five were demoted to a parent article, and 38 lost their link rather than keep a wrong one. A further 159 sights too obscure for any article, mostly Cornish coves, Hebridean heritage centres and one-word National Trust properties, were typed by hand. The Isle of Man is grouped with Northern England, as it is in the source chapters. Map links are searches, not pins: Lonely Planet publishes no coordinates.
