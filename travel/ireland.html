The eleven eras, by number of entries

Built from Lonely Planet’s own points-of-interest indexes — 929 listings for Ireland and 193 for Northern Ireland — across 308 destinations in all 32 counties. Ulster is the nine-county province, so Donegal, Cavan and Monaghan sit here with the six counties of Northern Ireland and the five regions cover the island without gaps; the Jurisdiction filter separates Ireland from Northern Ireland wherever the political line is the one you want. Dublin takes in Dalkey and Sandycove as well as Lonely Planet’s own Dublin chapter, which already includes Howth and Malahide, while Bray stays in Leinster as County Wicklow. All four inscribed World Heritage properties on the island are tagged: Brú na Bóinne and Sceilg Mhichíl in the Republic, the Giant’s Causeway and the Gracehill component of the transnational Moravian Church Settlements in the North. Gracehill has no Lonely Planet listing of its own and was added as a row marked “not in Lonely Planet”. All four of Ireland’s current tentative-list entries are represented too — the Royal Sites, the Transatlantic Cable Ensemble at Valentia, the Passage Tomb Landscape of County Sligo and the Historic Astronomical Observatories — and none of the United Kingdom’s five tentative entries has a component in Northern Ireland now that Gracehill is inscribed. Neither state has ever had a property delisted, so there is no former-site category here. Ireland’s tentative list was cut back in 2022–23, which is why the Burren, the Céide Fields, Clonmacnoise, the Western Stone Forts and the historic city of Dublin carry no tag although they are all in the table. On the eras: a sight sits in the band its founding year falls in, not the band of the fabric standing today — Dublin Castle is Anglo-Norman for King John’s castle of 1204 rather than Protestant Ascendancy for its Georgian ranges. One exception is documented: the Rock of Cashel is dated to Cormac’s Chapel and the round tower, about 1100, rather than to the fourth-century royal seat, because nothing of the royal site stands. The Viking band carries more than Norse Dublin and Waterford — the round towers, the high crosses and the first cathedrals are mostly tenth- to twelfth-century, so they land here — and for Northern Ireland the Republic band (1922–1998) is a period rather than a jurisdiction. The thinnest bands are reported rather than hidden: Iron Age carries 18 entries and Prehistory 25, which says more about what Lonely Planet indexes than about Irish archaeology, since the ringforts, cashels and court tombs outside the famous complexes mostly go unlisted. 429 entries take their era from their type alone, so these estimates matter: an undated museum, gallery or visitor centre is Republic; an undated church or chapel is Union with Great Britain, right for most Irish parish churches and wrong for the medieval ones; an undated castle, cathedral, abbey or tower house is Anglo-Norman; an undated country house, square, canal or civic building is Protestant Ascendancy; an undated ringfort or cashel is Iron Age; an undated round tower or high cross is Viking. A further 175 natural features are Natural by type. Of the rest, 186 eras come from a Wikidata date, 207 from the opening paragraph of the sight’s own article, 46 from a period cue, and 79 were set by hand. Wikipedia was resolved in four waves — direct lookup, then the title qualified by town and county, then a full-text search, then spelling variants such as Sliabh Liag for Slieve League — and every candidate was checked against its own opening paragraph for an Irish cue. That is what caught the House of Waterford Crystal resolving to Waterford, Connecticut, Sligo’s Model to the mathematical sense of the word, and namesakes for Baltimore, Saul, Ferns and New Quay; thirteen further picks were repaired by hand, including a Christ Church Cathedral that had landed on St Fin Barre’s in Cork. Where a sight has no article of its own the link points at the article that covers it — its town, its island or the larger monument — and the W badge is lower case. Two dating traps are worth recording: a saint’s name is not a date, since St Patrick’s in a title dated eight nineteenth-century churches and colleges to the fifth century until the cue was removed; and the word cashel means a stone fort, which put the Rock of Cashel and a folk village in the Iron Age. Map links are searches, not pins: Lonely Planet publishes no coordinates.
